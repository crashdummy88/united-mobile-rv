Safety · corridor travel

RV emergency roadside kit

Most kits are built around what is convenient to sell. Build yours around what you actually reach for on a shoulder — visibility first, then the tools that get you to a shop instead of stranded.

Active corridor: Montana · Wyoming · Idaho · Washington — case-by-case beyond.

Field notes

On remote MT · WY · ID · WA legs, the failure that strands you is often small — a soft tire, a dead chassis battery, a fuse, a dark shoulder. The kit that matters is the one you can find in the dark with one hand while traffic is still moving.

Who this is for

Right place if you

Are stocking a coach or tow vehicle for long corridor days. Inherited a “kit” that is mostly expired plastic. Want a priority order, not a shopping-aisle dump.

Maybe not yet if

You need tire pressure method — see tire pressure by load. You need fire extinguisher placement — see RV fire safety.

Visibility and scene safety first

Be seen

Reflective triangles or appropriate warning devices, a high-visibility vest, and a headlamp (hands-free matters). Place devices well back for the speed of the road you are on.

People first

A first-aid kit sized for who is traveling — not a token tin — plus personal meds kept accessible, not buried in a bay.

Weather layer

Gloves, rain shell, and drinking water beat another novelty multitool when you are outside for an hour.

Where you stop

If you can limp to a wide pullout or exit, do. Working in a live lane is a last resort.

Tools and communication

Match fasteners to your rig — a basic socket/hex set, screwdrivers, pliers, electrical tape, zip ties, and a roll of quality tape for temporary holds that get you to a shop. Tire plug kit and a portable compressor you have tested. Jumper cables or a jump pack rated for your chassis battery chemistry. Spare fuses and bulbs for the circuits you actually run. A paper map for the corridor you drive — cell coverage fails exactly where breakdowns hurt most. A properly installed cell booster (see weBoost install) improves the odds of a call getting out; Starlink helps when you already have a travel install — we do Starlink installs only, not as a “certified partner” title.

Owner checklist

  • Triangles/vest/headlamp staged where the driver can reach them first
  • Compressor and plug kit tested at home, not discovered empty on the shoulder
  • Jump pack charged on a schedule; cables uncoiled and checked
  • Paper maps for your usual corridor plus ice/water
  • Extinguisher accessible and within date — see fire-safety guide
  • Emergency contacts written down, not only in a locked phone

Failure modes we see

Kit buried

Perfect inventory under three totes and a grill.

Dead compressor

Never tested; battery flat; wrong fittings.

Phone-only navigation

No signal, no map, no plan.

No scene control

Working dark without triangles on a fast road.

DIY vs book UMRT

Reasonable DIY

Build and stage the kit, test air and jump gear, keep extinguishers current.
